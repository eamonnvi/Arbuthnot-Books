Christina Koning's Variable Stars is an ambitious, passionate work of historical fiction that follows the intertwined lives of a handful of late‑18th‑ and early‑19th century figures at the intersection of domestic life, music and the rapid advances being made at that time in the science of astronomy. It has a rare combination of technical verisimilitude and humane sympathy: it describes the craft of telescope making in the same breath as the small acts of care that constitute a life. 

At its emotional centre is Caroline (Carolina/Lina) Herschel: a woman who begins life as a household drudge in Hannover, becomes a singer and, by force of circumstance and singular industry, the practical partner and sometime scientific collaborator of her brother, the astronomer William Herschel. Surrounding her are a constellation of secondary figures—William himself, brilliant and single‑minded; Edward Pigott, an itinerant astronomer and the love of Caroline’s life; John Goodricke, the prodigious, deaf observer whose early death is a tragic pivot; and Mathurina (Mathurine) Pigott, whose secret love and fateful choices cast long shadows.

The novel is rich in period detail and atmosphere. Everyday life—food, clothing, domestic hierarchy, musical practice, the physical labor of mirror‑grinding and telescope construction are rendered vividly. Early 19th-century Bath’s social rituals, London’s theatres and the rhythms of provincial life are all evoked with authority.

For those interested in the history of science, the details of astronomical work—the methodical scanning of the sky at night, eyepieces, mirror‑grinding, listing nebulae, and the exhilaration of a first sighting—is are brought to life.

But science here is both liberating and demanding: Caroline’s access to observational instruments grants her intellectual agency, yet it also ties her to a labouring life that denies other possibilities (formal education, marriage).

Astronomy is alos presented as a metaphor for time itself: stars are both ancient and immediate; observing them is a way of apprehending the past. The principal characters are haunted by memory; the novel’s later sections become an archive of loss as much as of discovery.

All the characters are vividly drawn, but none more so than Caroline Herschel. Where many historical novels flatten women into companions to charismatic men, Variable Stars invests in Caroline’s interior life: her appetite for learning, love of music, experience of shame and scarring from smallpox, and her sacrifices. As apprentice astronomer, singer, assistant, discoverer, and ultimately the keeper of the archive, her story is the moral spine of the narrative.
